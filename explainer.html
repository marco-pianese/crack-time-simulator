01  What a website does with your password

It doesn't keep it

When you sign up somewhere, a competent website does not store the password you chose. It stores a hash of it: a fingerprint produced by a one-way function. Easy to compute in one direction, impossible to run backwards.

Hash — a one-way fingerprint
MD5("cat") = d077f244def8a70e5ea758bd8352fcd8 MD5("cot") = 97223fab7b0d4c64c07e6e004c602302 MD5("correct horse") = 3cb4e732631f47e6eb961f34554b7cde

Change one letter and the result has nothing in common with the previous one. There is no way to look at d077f2… and work out that it came from "cat". When you log in, the website fingerprints whatever you typed and compares the two fingerprints — it never needs the original.

So an attacker who steals the database gets a list of fingerprints, not passwords. Their only option is to guess: fingerprint "cat", fingerprint "cot", fingerprint a billion others, and watch for a match. That guessing loop is the only thing the simulator measures. Everything else on this page is about what makes the loop fast or slow.

Salt — a random string mixed in first
MD5("cat") = d077f244def8a70e5ea758bd8352fcd8 MD5("7fK2qR" + "cat") = b0278d78c403b5ad500ad799005ea446

A salt is a random string the website adds to your password before fingerprinting, different for every user. It isn't secret — it sits in the database right next to the hash.

Its job is to break bulk attacks. Without a salt, everyone who chose password123 has an identical fingerprint, so an attacker tests one guess against the entire database at once — or simply looks the fingerprint up in a precomputed table of common passwords. With a unique salt per user, every single account has to be attacked separately. The five fast algorithms at the top of the simulator's table are usually stored without one.

The part that produces the enormous spread

MD5 and SHA-256 were designed to be fast, because their original job was checking that a downloaded file arrived intact — you want that to take microseconds. Speed is exactly the wrong property for storing passwords, because the attacker gets the same speed.

So bcrypt, scrypt and Argon2id are built to be deliberately, pointlessly slow. They repeat the work thousands of times over. Argon2id additionally demands tens of megabytes of memory for every single guess, which graphics cards are bad at supplying — that one design choice removes most of a cracking rig's advantage.

Work factor — the slowness dial
bcrypt cost 5 = 2⁵ = 32 rounds ← benchmark setting, too weak for real use bcrypt cost 10 = 2¹⁰ = 1,024 rounds ← 32× slower, OWASP's minimum bcrypt cost 12 = 2¹² = 4,096 rounds ← 128× slower than cost 5

The dial has to be set somewhere. A website tunes it so that verifying one login takes a fraction of a second — fast enough that you don't notice, slow enough that an attacker making billions of attempts is reduced to thousands. OWASP's guidance is to raise it as high as the server tolerates while staying under a second per login.

This is the lever you don't control. Your password's unpredictability is your decision. The storage algorithm and its work factor are the website's decision, you are never told what they chose, and between the top and bottom of the simulator's table there are roughly eight orders of magnitude — a factor of a hundred million.

02  The arithmetic, worked through

Where the simulator's numbers come from

One example, start to finish, with every intermediate value written out. The password is brightwinterlamp — sixteen lowercase letters, assumed to have been chosen at random. Follow it once and every figure in the simulator becomes readable.

1

Count the options for one character

Only lowercase letters are used, so each position holds one of 26 possibilities. Had we used uppercase and digits as well it would be 26 + 26 + 10 = 62.

options per position = 26
2

Count every password it could have been

Sixteen positions, 26 independent choices each. Multiply 26 by itself sixteen times.

26¹⁶ = 43,608,742,899,428,874,059,776 ≈ 44 thousand billion billion possibilities
3

Rewrite it as bits, so it fits on a screen

Twenty-three digits is unreadable. Bits express the same quantity as a power of two — how many times you would have to double 1 to reach it. That is all log₂ means.

log₂(43,608,742,899,428,874,059,776) = 75.2 bits the shortcut: 16 characters × log₂(26) = 16 × 4.7 = 75.2

This is the Textbook entropy figure in the simulator.

4

Halve it, because the attacker gets lucky halfway

An attacker working through the list doesn't have to reach the end. On average they find the answer after checking half of it. So the number of guesses to expect is half the total.

43,608,742,899,428,874,059,776 ÷ 2 = 21,804,371,449,714,437,029,888 guesses

The pattern-aware column skips this halving, because zxcvbn already reports an expected number of guesses rather than a total count. Halving it twice would be a factor-of-two error, and it's a common one in tools like this.

5

Divide by the attacker's speed

Here is where the website's storage choice takes over. Same password, same attacker, two different decisions made by whoever built the site.

stored with MD5 — 220,600,000,000 guesses/sec 21,804,371,449,714,437,029,888 ÷ 220,600,000,000 = 98,841,212,374 seconds stored with bcrypt cost 10 — 9,500 guesses/sec 21,804,371,449,714,437,029,888 ÷ 9,500 = 2,295,196,994,706,782,720 seconds
6

Turn seconds into something human

A year is 31,557,600 seconds. Divide.

MD5 98,841,212,374 ÷ 31,557,600 = ≈ 3,100 years bcrypt cost 10 2,295,196,994,706,782,720 ÷ 31,557,600 = ≈ 73 billion years

Both figures appear in the simulator whenever the attacker is set to a single graphics card. The spread between them came from one decision, made by someone you never met.

Live check — the three reference cases, recomputed in your browser

These three cases are written down in the project's research notes. The page recalculates them from scratch each time it loads. If they ever stop matching the notes, something in the code has broken — a cheap regression test in public view.

Randomly generated passwordEntropyAgainst MD5Against bcrypt cost 10

One high-end graphics card. All arithmetic is performed on base-10 exponents rather than the numbers themselves, so a very long password does not overflow to infinity.

03  Two different attacks

Stolen database, not login screen

Every number in the simulator assumes one specific situation. Getting this wrong makes the figures frightening in the wrong direction.

Offline — what the simulator measures

The company was breached and the password file is sitting on the attacker's hard drive. Nobody can slow them down, lock them out, or even notice. They guess as fast as their hardware allows, for as long as they feel like.

Limited by
The attacker's hardware budget
Speed
Thousands to hundreds of billions of guesses per second
Your defence
Real unpredictability, plus a slow algorithm you didn't choose

Online — your actual login screen

Somebody is typing guesses into the real website. After a handful of failures they hit a lockout, a CAPTCHA, or a two-factor prompt. They get dozens of attempts, not billions.

Limited by
Rate limiting, lockout, CAPTCHA, two-factor
Speed
A few dozen attempts in total
Your defence
Simply not being on a list of known passwords

A mediocre password survives the online attack comfortably. It is the offline case — the one that happens after a breach you had no part in and no warning of — where the figures in the simulator apply.

04  Can you find out?

Mostly no, and that's the whole problem

If the storage algorithm decides whether your password falls in hours or holds for millennia, the obvious question is whether you can discover which one a website uses. No law requires disclosure — not even GDPR — and most sites say nothing. But four routes give partial answers.

1 · Some companies publish it

A minority document their scheme openly. Dropbox has a detailed engineering write-up of theirs. OWASP's own guidance encourages disclosure and points to a crowd-sourced registry where such statements get collected. Security researchers have argued for years that disclosure should be mandatory on the signup page, on the grounds that companies would rather fix weak storage than publicly admit to it.

2 · The software stack gives it away

Almost nobody writes their own password handling — they use the framework default, and those defaults are public knowledge. The stack is often visible from outside, too: a /wp-login.php page tells you it's WordPress.

PlatformDefault storage
WordPress 6.8 and laterbcrypt, with SHA-384 pre-hashing
WordPress before 6.8phpass — the old scheme, much weaker
DjangoPBKDF2-SHA256
Ruby on Railsbcrypt

WordPress is a useful illustration of how long these transitions take: the change to bcrypt shipped in 2025, and existing passwords are only upgraded the next time each user logs in. A site running an older version, or a dormant account, still carries the old scheme.

3 · Behavioural tells — the ones you can check tonight

These are mostly negative signals, and some are conclusive.

✗The site emails you your password. Conclusive, and the worst case. If they can send it to you, they never hashed it. It is sitting in their database in plaintext or in reversible encryption.
✗A low maximum length, like 16 or 20 characters. Hashing produces fixed-length output no matter how long the input is, so a cap suggests the password is being stored in a sized database field rather than hashed.
✗Spaces or certain symbols rejected. A hash function doesn't care what the characters are. Restrictions suggest something is parsing or escaping your password instead of simply hashing it.
✗"Your new password must differ from the old one by more than two characters." They can only check that by comparing against something they can still read.
✗Login works with the wrong capitalisation. Something is normalising your password before storage — usually a sign of an old system.
✓A maximum of exactly 72 characters. The opposite signal. That is bcrypt's input limit, so the cap is evidence that bcrypt is in use.
✓No composition rules, long passphrases accepted, and a breach-list check at signup. This is current NIST guidance, and following it suggests somebody is paying attention.

4 · After a breach, you find out

The algorithm usually becomes public in the aftermath, named in the disclosure or worked out by researchers from the dump itself. Too late to act on, but it is where the simulator's figures stop being theoretical — which is the subject of the next section.

The practical conclusion is uncomfortable. For most websites you use, you cannot know, and you cannot influence it. Which means the only sane assumption is that at least one of them is doing it badly — and the only defence that survives that assumption is a different password on every site.

05  One breach, both ways

The same stolen database, two storage methods

The simulator proves its point with arithmetic. This incident proves it with history, and it is the cleanest natural experiment available, because both methods appear in the same stolen file.

In 2015 the dating site Ashley Madison was breached — nearly 40 million records. The site had used bcrypt, the right choice for the time. But a programming fault meant that a large number of passwords had also been stored using a single round of MD5.

So the same attackers, working on the same data with the same hardware, faced both storage methods at once:

Stored withResultEffort
bcryptAbout 4,000 passwords recoveredOne researcher, one week
MD5 (the bug)Over 11 million passwords recoveredA team, about ten days

Figures as reported at the time. The bcrypt figure is one researcher's effort rather than a controlled comparison, so treat the ratio as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. S

Roughly a factor of a million in effectiveness, from a storage decision no user could see, discover, or influence. Note what did not change between those two rows: not the passwords, not the attackers, not the hardware. Only the function the site had chosen to run.

There is a second lesson in it. bcrypt did its job — the accounts stored that way largely held. What defeated it was a bug that routed passwords around it. Good cryptography fails through implementation mistakes far more often than through anybody breaking the mathematics, which is worth remembering when you reach the quantum section.

06  What quantum computing changes

Two questions, two very different answers

Almost everything written about quantum computing and security collapses two separate questions into one, which is why the subject feels confusing. Separate them and it becomes straightforward.

Could a quantum computer break the padlock in my browser? Yes — completely Not weakened, broken. This is a genuine threat, taken seriously enough that replacement standards already exist with deadlines attached. The algorithm for this is called Shor
Could a quantum computer guess my password faster? Barely — probably never A modest theoretical speed-up that collapses when you try to build it. Today's graphics card is the better attacker, and will likely stay that way. The algorithm for this is called Grover

Why the answers differ: solve versus search

The reason one answer is catastrophic and the other is a shrug has nothing to do with quantum physics. It's about what kind of protection each thing uses.

Solve

Some protection rests on a puzzle. The padlock in your browser works because a particular piece of arithmetic is easy to do forwards and ruinously slow to undo. Nothing is being guessed — the security is entirely in that asymmetry.

If someone finds a fast way to undo it, the lock doesn't get weaker. It stops being a lock. There is no amount of "make the puzzle bigger" that survives a method which simply solves puzzles.

Used by
RSA, elliptic curves — the browser padlock, digital signatures
Quantum
Shor's algorithm solves exactly this kind of puzzle
Outcome
Total break
Search

Other protection rests on a list. Your password hash contains no puzzle and no hidden shortcut. The only way in is to try candidates one after another and watch for a match — exactly what the simulator counts.

A quantum computer can search a list somewhat faster. But faster searching is still searching: you can make the list longer, and the defence keeps working. There is nothing to solve.

Used by
Password hashes, AES, SHA-2 — stored passwords, encrypted files
Quantum
Grover's algorithm searches lists a bit faster
Outcome
A modest dent, and probably not even that

If you remember one sentence from this section: Shor solves, Grover searches. Your password is protected by a list, not a puzzle — so it faces the weak attack, not the devastating one.

Before either: what a qubit actually is, and why counting them misleads

Qubit — and the thousand-to-one problem

An ordinary computer bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can hold a blend of both at once, which is what lets certain algorithms explore many possibilities in a single pass.

The catch is fragility. Qubits are corrupted constantly by heat, vibration and stray radiation. So you bundle many unreliable physical qubits together and run error correction across them to simulate one trustworthy logical qubit. That exchange rate is currently around a thousand to one.

~1,000 physical qubits ≈ 1 logical qubit usable for real computation best demonstrations so far about 100 logical qubits needed to break the padlock thousands of logical qubits

This is why a headline announcing "1,000 qubits" tells you much less than it sounds like. The question to ask is how many logical ones.

The padlock question, in detail

When your browser shows a padlock, it has just agreed a shared secret with the server using maths that is cheap one way and brutal in reverse. Shor's algorithm removes the brutality. The lock doesn't bend; it evaporates.

That threat is real enough that the replacement standards are already published. Here is how the estimated cost of actually doing it has moved:

YearEstimated cost of breaking RSA-2048
2012About a billion noisy physical qubitsS
2019About 20 million noisy qubits, running 8 hoursP
2025Under 1 million noisy qubits, running under a weekP

Read that table carefully, because it is easy to misread in both directions. It is not evidence that the machine is about to arrive. It is evidence that the estimate keeps moving, always the same way. The twentyfold drop between 2019 and 2025 came from cleverer algorithms and better error correction, not from new hardware.

Against that: the largest number ever factored by Shor's algorithm is 21. About 100 logical qubits exist; thousands are needed. The gap is roughly four orders of magnitude, and nothing we read supports naming a year.

The password question, in detail

This is the part that gets mangled, so here it is as a picture you can hold onto.

A

A corridor of lockers

Picture one locker for every password the attacker might try, with the answer inside exactly one of them. An ordinary computer opens them one at a time. A billion possible passwords means up to a billion lockers.

B

What Grover's algorithm promises

Grover finds the right locker in roughly the square root of the number of checks. A billion lockers becomes about thirty-two thousand. That sounds devastating, and it is the entire basis of "quantum halves your security".

ordinary computer 1,000,000,000 checks Grover √1,000,000,000 = 31,623 checks
C

But you can't buy more of them — the catch that matters

Ordinary cracking scales by purchase. Two machines, half the time. A thousand machines, a thousandth of the time. That is precisely what makes the hardware in the simulator frightening, and why the thousand-card row is so much worse than the laptop.

Grover doesn't work like that. Split the corridor between machines and each one still has to do far more than its fair share, and the total effort grows instead of staying flat. The strategy that makes ordinary attacks scale simply stops working.

ordinary, 100 machines total work unchanged, finishes 100× sooner Grover, 100 machines total work 10× larger, finishes only 10× sooner
D

And every check is desperately slow

A Grover "check" is not a quick glance into a locker. It means running the entire scrambling algorithm backwards inside a fragile quantum circuit, wrapped in error correction. Those operations are vastly slower than an ordinary computer's, and by step C they must happen one after another.

Put a generous number on it. Imagine a machine managing a billion Grover steps per second — far beyond anything demonstrated or on any roadmap. Attacking a 128-bit key takes about 2⁶⁴ steps:

2⁶⁴ steps = 18,446,744,073,709,551,616 ÷ 1,000,000,000 steps per second = 18,446,744,073 seconds = 585 years, in sequence, on one machine

This is why NIST still treats AES-128 as secure, and in fact uses it as the yardstick for measuring the new post-quantum algorithms. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre reached the same conclusion: 256-bit ciphers and hash functions stay safe against Grover.

E

And passwords are a worse target than that

Everything above concerns tidy 128-bit keys. Password storage is harder for Grover, not easier, because the machine would have to run Argon2id itself backwards inside the quantum circuit — and Argon2id deliberately demands tens of megabytes of working memory per guess. Holding that in quantum superposition is not something anyone is approaching.

Meanwhile the €2,000 graphics card in the simulator makes 220 billion ordinary guesses a second, today, and scales perfectly if you buy a second one. Against password storage specifically, a quantum computer would be the worse attacker.

This last point is our own reasoning from the published arguments about AES, not something we found stated in a source. It is flagged the same way in the project's research notes, and it needs expert review before anyone repeats it.

So what is actually worth worrying about

The real quantum risk has a name: harvest now, decrypt later. An adversary records encrypted traffic today, stores it, and decrypts it in fifteen years when a sufficient machine exists. It works because much of what crosses the network now — medical records, state communications, trade secrets — is still sensitive decades from now.

Notice what it doesn't threaten. A recorded conversation does not become a login. This attacks the padlock on the channel, which is the solve column, and your password lives in the search column.

MilestoneStatus
FIPS 203, 204, 205Replacement standards approved, 13 August 2024
HQCSelected as a fifth algorithm, 11 March 2025
RSA and elliptic curvesTo be deprecated after 2030, disallowed after 2035

That is a migration happening in protocols and standards bodies, on a published schedule. It is not something an individual acts on, and nothing about how you choose passwords should change because of it.

Claims this project refuses to make

✗"Quantum computers will crack any password in seconds." No source supports this.
✗"Grover halves your security, so double your password length." The halving is a bound on the number of queries, not an attack anybody can run. The advice doesn't change.
✗"RSA-2048 has already been broken in secret." The largest number factored by Shor's algorithm is 21.
✗"Machine X has N qubits, so we're nearly there." Physical qubits are not logical qubits. Ask for the logical count.
✗Any specific date for when this lands. Nothing we read supports one.
07  What to do about it

Five things that follow from the numbers

Not generic advice. Each one is a direct consequence of something on this page.

01

A different password everywhere, which means a password manager

Section 04 is the argument: you cannot find out how a website stores your password, so assume at least one of them does it badly. Unique passwords stop that company's mistake from unlocking everything else you own. Forty unique passwords is impossible to remember, so a manager isn't a luxury here — it's what makes the rest achievable.

02

Length beats symbol soup

The two figures in the simulator show why. Every extra character multiplies the attacker's work, while swapping a for @ adds almost nothing because that substitution is the first thing they try. Four or five unrelated words beat P@ssw0rd! by an enormous margin and are far easier to type.

03

Two-factor authentication where it matters

Section 03 separates online from offline attacks. Two-factor is what keeps the online column harmless, and it also limits the damage when a leaked password turns out to have been reused. Email, banking and your password manager first.

04

Check whether your passwords have already leaked

The pattern-aware figure drops hardest for passwords that have appeared in a breach, because those are the very first thing an attacker tries. Have I Been Pwned searches known breaches, and most password managers now do this continuously.

05

Don't reorganise your life around quantum computing

Section 06 is the argument. Your password is protected by a list, not a puzzle, so it faces the weak quantum attack. The migration that matters is happening in standards bodies on a schedule running to 2035, and it is not something you act on personally.
